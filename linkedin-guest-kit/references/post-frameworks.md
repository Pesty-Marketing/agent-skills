# LinkedIn Post Frameworks

Operative rules for writing a LinkedIn feed post for a podcast guest — a founder posting to professional peers, not a brand chasing viral reach.

## 1. Hook types

The hook is everything before LinkedIn's "see more" fold — roughly the **first 1–2 lines on mobile, 3–5 on desktop**. Nothing after it gets read unless the hook earns the click; write it first, alone. These types, from Demand Curve's analysis of top posts, recur; stacking two or three in one hook (Fear + Surprise + Value, say) beats a single mechanism.

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Credibility | Proven result or borrowed authority | "I run two 7-figure businesses. Here's what almost killed both." |
| Value | Plain-language payoff, upfront | "How to cut technician turnover in half in 90 days." |
| Curiosity | Open loop the reader must close | "I turned down a $2M offer. Here's why." |
| Counter-narrative | Contradicts a held belief | "Everyone says hire for culture fit. That nearly sank us." |
| Fear | Loss aversion / left-behind, outdone | "One bad hire from losing your best client — most are." |
| Surprise | Self-contained shocking stat | "73% of our leads came from a channel we almost cut." |
| Identity | Barnum statement or direct label | "If you're a founder who still answers your own tickets—" |
| Eloquence | Names an unspoken frustration | "Nobody tells you growth makes you lonelier, not less." |
| Celebration | Milestone inviting an easy "congrats" | "Five years ago this was a garage. Today: 100 employees." |
| Faces (visual) | Expressive, matched photo | Match photo expression to the hook's emotion — never a stock headshot. |

## 2. The hook/clickbait line

**A hook teases the value the post is about to deliver. Clickbait teases value and never delivers it.** Every hook claim must be true and match what the body actually contains — an inflated promise ("I'll teach you more than a 4-year degree") reads as clickbait and burns trust, the one currency that compounds here.

## 3. Why people share

| Driver | The reader's thought | Write to trigger it |
|---|---|---|
| Utility | "My network should see this" | Make the post reusable — a checklist or specific fix, not just an anecdote. |
| Belief articulation | "They said what I couldn't" | State the unspoken frustration plainly and early; don't hedge it away. |
| Social signaling | "Sharing this says who I am" | Give the post one clear, quotable stance to attach a name to. |

## 4. Post structure

1. **Hook** (1–2 lines) — one type from Section 1, alone.
2. **Context** — 1–3 lines bridging hook to story; most posts fail here by skipping to the pitch.
3. **Body** — the substance. Default to **problem → solution**: the audience's real problem, then the fix, leading with their benefit, not your accomplishment.
4. **Zinger** — one tight, quotable closing insight.
5. **CTA** — one explicit ask (comment, share, follow); ask a real question rather than just broadcast.

## 5. Formatting rules

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Paragraphs | 1–2 sentences; 60%+ of traffic is mobile. |
| Line breaks | After nearly every sentence — white space drives skimmability, not shorter content. |
| Bullets/numbers | Break up lists for scannability. |
| Emoji | Fine in moderation for tone/section breaks; don't let them replace real formatting. |
| Hashtags | Optional, 2–3 max — moves reach by low single digits, not the multiplier assumed. |
| Language | Plain over jargon, ~11–12-year-old level; active voice, specific verbs. |
| Links | Never in the initial post body — see Timing below. |
| Images | Include one; a relevant photo (especially a face) out-engages text-only. |

## 6. Length

**The sources give no usable feed-post word count, and one figure must be discounted.** A "3,000-post analysis" claims "1,900–2,000 words" maximizes reach and "1,100–1,200" maximizes backlinks. Both are impossible for a feed post — the composer caps near 3,000 characters (~500 words) — so this can only describe LinkedIn Articles (the separate long-form feature) or blog content. The source never names "Articles" or flags the distinction; treat the number as **not applicable to feed posts**.

On the feed itself, the sources say: short posts, "often under 250 words," work well for a quick win or tip; and "super short form doesn't work on LinkedIn" the way it does on X — a post needs enough copy to trigger "see more," since that click is itself a ranked signal. Video: under 30 seconds wins for brand awareness (200% higher completion); longer suits demand-gen storytelling, but the first 10 seconds decide retention either way.

**Default:** several short paragraphs, long enough to trigger "see more," well under 250 words unless the story needs more room. Never target four-figure counts for a feed post.

## 7. Timing and mechanics

- **Days:** Tuesday–Thursday. **Times:** two overlapping windows in the sources — 7–10am, and 10/11am, 12, 2, 3pm — audience timezone; either beats weekends or evenings.
- **Frequency:** at most once/day; 3–5 posts/week is typical outside full-time creators. Two posts within 18 hours split each other's reach.
- **Reply fast:** the first 8 hours' reach hinges on engagement in the first 90 minutes — reply then, and again a few hours later. Substantive replies (12+ words) beat "Thanks!"
- **Never be the first commenter** on your own post — a reach killer.
- **Post first, add the link later** (per Josh Spector's analysis of Justin Welsh's activity): publish without a link, let the algorithm score it for roughly an hour, then edit the link in; drop it in a comment if it's needed sooner.
- Native video and documents (PDFs/carousels) only — YouTube and off-platform links get suppressed.

## 8. Guest-post-specific guidance (applied, not sourced)

None of the above targets turning a podcast appearance into a post — apply this judgment on top of it:

- **Write in the guest's voice, not the host's marketing voice.** A draft that could've come from the show's producer is wrong; pull real phrases and specifics from what the guest actually said.
- **Lead with the guest's expertise, not the fact of the appearance.** "I was on a podcast" is the least interesting hook available — an announcement, not a value promise, and it fails the Section 2 test by default. Open with the guest's strongest claim, then reveal it came from the episode.
- **Credit the host without making the host the hero.** Name the show and host once, naturally, often near the CTA — a courtesy, not the reason the post exists.
- **The episode is context, not content.** Problem → solution still applies: lead with the audience's problem, not "here's what I talked about."

## Anti-patterns

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Hook promises what the body doesn't deliver | Match the hook to what the post actually contains |
| "I was a guest on [podcast]" as the opener | Lead with the guest's strongest claim; mention the show later |
| Targeting ~2,000 words "because research says so" | That figure is for Articles, not feed posts — stay short-to-medium |
| Posting a raw outbound link at publish | Add it via edit ~1 hour later, or drop it in a comment |
| Wishy-washy, hedge-everything statements | Take one clear stance the audience can agree or disagree with |
| Generic stock-photo headshot | Use a real photo whose expression matches the hook |
